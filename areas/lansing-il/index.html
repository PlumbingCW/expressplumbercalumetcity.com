Older homes, older pipes: plumbing in Lansing

Lansing is a Cook County village of about 28,100 residents on the Illinois and Indiana state line. A 2025 water affordability plan for the village, published through the University of Illinois Chicago, reports that 39 percent of Lansing homes were built in 1959 or earlier and 58 percent between 1960 and 1999. For a plumber, that mix means galvanized steel, copper and some lead in the walls, and clay or cast iron in the ground.

The same plan says much of the village's older housing was built with lead plumbing, and that Lansing is working with the Illinois EPA on low-interest loans to replace lead service lines. If your house is from the older group, a burst or leak is often the moment to look at what the rest of the pipe is made of.

Pipes from the older stock

Homes from 1959 and earlier commonly have galvanized supply lines and clay or cast iron drains. Those materials corrode and crack, and they are the usual suspects behind low pressure and repeat backups.

Water from Hammond

Lansing's water department buys Lake Michigan water from the Hammond Water Works Department, according to the village plan. That is the same Lake Michigan source behind most of Calumet City's supply.

A river nearby

The Little Calumet River levee protects Lansing and Calumet City together. Reports in 2023 said the two communities received nearly $15 million from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers to clear vegetation and rebuild the levee.

  • ✓Turn the main water valve clockwise until it stops, then open the lowest faucet to drain the lines

What Lansing homeowners call about most

A Lansing local note: water, lead and your service line

Lansing local note

The village plan describes Lansing as selling Hammond water to its own residents and to the villages of Lynwood and South Holland, under what its staff call a long-term supply agreement. If you want your water tested, or want to know what your service line is made of, the village water department is the right first call.
