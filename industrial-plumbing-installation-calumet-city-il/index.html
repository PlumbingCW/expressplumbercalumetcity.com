What counts as an industrial or commercial install

An installation in a commercial or light industrial building usually means larger pipe sizes, more fixtures, floor drains, grease or sand interceptors, backflow assemblies and sometimes process water or compressed air lines. It also means more rules, because the building affects employees, customers and the public water system.

This guide covers the planning questions. For repairs and after-hours problems, see our commercial plumbing page. For installs, the work is scheduled, and the plumber you hire matters.

Common elements of a commercial plumbing scope

Restrooms and fixtures

Counts and accessibility requirements come from the building code and the use of the space.

Floor drains and sumps

Process areas and mechanical rooms need floor drains, and low areas may need a sump or ejector.

Grease and sand interceptors

Restaurants and car washes need interceptors, and local rules govern sizing and maintenance.

Water heating

Commercial heaters are sized to peak demand and may be gas, electric or steam-fed.

Backflow prevention

Assemblies protect the public water system and typically need periodic testing.

Gas piping

Gas work has its own rules and safety checks.

Planning for tenants, shared walls and shutoffs

In a multi-tenant building or a shared warehouse, one tenant's shutoff can cut water to a neighbor. Before the plumber isolates a line, find out who else is on it, who controls the valves and how much notice the landlord or property manager requires. A written notice a few days ahead prevents most of the arguments.

Ask where the main shutoff and any backflow assembly are located, and whether the valves still turn. A seized valve discovered on the day of the job turns a one-hour shutoff into a half-day delay. If a valve fails, the plumber may need to shut water off at the street, and that is a utility matter, so ask early who to call.

Drains, slope and floor work in shops and warehouses

Concrete floors with drains need the right slope so water reaches the drain instead of pooling. If you are adding a wash area, a floor drain or a new sink in an existing slab, the plumber will cut the concrete, run the drain line, set the trap and patch the floor. Ask who patches and finishes the concrete, because that line item is often missing from a first bid.

Think about what goes down the drain. Oil, solvents, wash water and food waste each have their own rules. A floor drain that connects to the sanitary sewer is not a place to dump anything you would not pour into a toilet, and some operations need an interceptor or a separate collection tank. Ask the local authority before you finalize the layout.

Testing, documentation and what to keep

At the end of the job, ask for the inspection sign-off, the test results for any backflow assembly and a simple sketch of what was installed and where the shutoffs are. Keep these with your building records. They save time the next time someone services the system, and an insurer or a buyer may ask for them later.

Calumet City local note: permits, licensing and the retail corridors

The Illinois Department of Public Health says that individuals who plan, inspect, install, alter, extend, repair and maintain plumbing in Illinois must be licensed. It adds that plumbing contractors must register with the state, pay an annual fee and carry specified insurance. Businesses must also display at least one licensed plumber's license at their place of business. IDPH offers an online verification tool.

Calumet City has commercial areas along 159th Street, Sibley Boulevard and Wentworth Avenue and around River Oaks, and building work there should start with the Calumet City Building and Zoning Department. Permit rules vary, so ask what applies to your use and your address before you sign a contract.

Cost factors

Scope, pipe size, trenching and concrete cutting, fixture count, interceptors, backflow assemblies, inspection fees and the schedule all drive price. A fixed scope with unit prices for extras helps you compare bids. After-hours work to avoid closing the business costs more.
